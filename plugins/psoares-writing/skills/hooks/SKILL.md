# Hooks

A hook has one job: stop the scroll and let the right viewer decide to stay. To decide, they
need two answers fast: **what is this about**, and **what do I get if I keep watching**. Curiosity
comes from **contrast**: the viewer believes A, the hook shows B, and they want to close the
gap.

This skill writes the opening. Everything after it comes from the skills that own it:

- **`storytelling`** for the structure of the rest of the video.
- **`video-script`** for one sentence per line, the breath limit, lists and hard words.
- **`human-prose`** (from `s2-writing`) for the voice.
- **`teleprompter-script`** (from `psoares-teleprompter`) when the script is read from the app.

## Modes

- **Write.** From a topic or a script to hook options. Follow "Writing hooks" below.
- **Fix.** A hook the user wrote, against the four mistakes in
  [references/review.md](references/review.md). Give the problem, then options. Keep the rest
  of the script as it is.
- **Review.** The opening of a script: hook, text on screen, visual, lock-in zone. Report what
  breaks, line by line. Ask before rewriting: the user's voice wins.

## Before writing, ask

Ask only what the notes do not already answer.

1. What is the video about, in one sentence, and who is it for?
2. What does that viewer want, or what are they struggling with?
3. Short-form or long-form? On which platform?
4. What can be on screen in the first seconds: the speaker only, the screen, b-roll?
5. Is there real proof: a number, a result, a client, a screenshot?
6. Is there a named method or term the video teaches?
7. What is the obvious reason a viewer would think "this isn't for me"?

## What every hook needs

All four, or it does not hook:

1. **Subject.** Who or what the video is about, clear at once.
2. **Stakes.** A reason to care: the outcome the viewer wants, or the pain they want gone. In
   entertainment, a situation they want to see resolved.
3. **Speed.** Subject and stakes in as few words as possible. Compressed, not rushed.
4. **One reading.** Simple words, no wordplay, no vague teaser. If the viewer has to think for
   half a second, they swipe.

## The formula

Two to four lines. Topic in the first, contrast in the next one or two.

1. **Lean in.** Say what the video is about and pull the viewer closer with common ground, a
   benefit or pain they have, a metaphor, or a fact that surprises them.
2. **Stop.** One line built on a turn: *but*, *however*, *yet*, "here's the thing".
3. **Snap back.** A line that goes the opposite way from the lean, still on topic. The bigger
   the turn, the stronger the hook.

> The tech in the Vegas Sphere is insane: the biggest screen ever built.
> But the screen is the least impressive part.
> The most impressive part is the audio.

The turn only works if what follows delivers. When the visual already carries the surprise
(something the viewer has never seen), the "but" is not needed: describe what they see.

Contrast can be **stated** (A and B both said: "Most people do X, but Y works three times
faster") or **implied** (only B said, because A is what everyone already does).

## Writing hooks

1. **Find the key visual.** What can be on screen in the first three to five seconds that
   shows the topic? With no good visual, make one (text, a screen, a graphic) or question the
   idea. A talking head can still win with a sharp spoken and text hook.
2. **List the angles** on the topic and pick the one with the most contrast.
3. **Pick formats.** Write three to five options, each in a different format from
   [references/formats.md](references/formats.md). Any topic fits any format.
4. **Write the spoken hook** for each, with the formula.
5. **Write the text hook and the visual** so all three say the same thing. Rules in
   [references/layers.md](references/layers.md).
6. **Write the lock-in zone**: the one to four lines after the hook that confirm it and earn
   trust. Methods in [references/lock-in.md](references/lock-in.md).
7. **Check each option** against [references/review.md](references/review.md), then
   recommend one and say why.

## Output, per option

```markdown
### Option 2: Contrarian

**Spoken**
Everyone says you should learn to use AI tools.
But the tools change every month.
What lasts is knowing what good work looks like.

**Lock-in** (rapid contrast)
…

**Text on screen**
Learn the craft
not the tools

**Visual**
…

**Why it works:** A = learn the tools (what they've heard), B = learn the craft. Implied
contrast with the usual advice.
```

Then a short note on which option to use and why. Write the hooks in the language of the
script. [references/examples.md](references/examples.md) has examples in English and PT-PT.

## Rules

- **Topic in the first sentence.** Cut any line before it.
- **Talk to "you".** "If you've struggled with…" beats "I've struggled with…".
- **Lead with the pain or the benefit,** then the subject: "If you want better sleep, you
  need magnesium".
- **Short sentences in the hook.** They force clarity. Vary the length after it.
- **Words, text and visual say the same thing.** With the sound off, the visual should
  suggest the words, and the other way round.
- **Something familiar around something new.** A complex idea lands better through a name,
  brand or event the viewer already knows.
- **Value early.** Give a first useful thing in the hook or right after it.
- **No invented proof.** Only use numbers, results and claims the user gave. Without proof,
  use another lock-in method.

## Long-form

In a long YouTube video, the hook and the lock-in zone are the intro, and they have to confirm
the title and the thumbnail. The viewer gives it a minute or two, not four seconds, so there
is room for a map of the video and a short "who I am" with real proof. The first line still
says what the video is about.

## What not to do

- Do not open with a teaser that says nothing: "You won't believe this", "Wait till you see
  this".
- Do not use jargon the viewer has to decode in the first lines.
- Do not copy a hook that worked for someone else. Take its format or its power words and
  remix them.
- Do not cite the psychology behind this as fact in the content itself. It is craft, not
  science.

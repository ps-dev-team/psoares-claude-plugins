# The layers of a hook

A hook is more than the words said. It has layers, and they have to say the same thing:

1. **Spoken hook.** What the speaker says.
2. **Text hook.** The words on screen that are not the captions.
3. **Visual hook.** What is shown.
4. **Audio.** Music and sound effects.
5. **Caption hook.** The first line of the post's caption, above the fold.

## Line them up

A viewer looks first, listens, then looks again to check that what they heard matches what
they see. If the words say one thing and the picture another, they lose the thread, and a
viewer who is not sure what the video is about leaves.

Test: watch the opening with the sound off. The visual and the text should let you guess the
words. Then listen without watching: the words should let you guess the picture.

Start from the visual: find the strongest shot for the first three to five seconds, pick the
format that fits it, then write the words to match it.

| Lined up | Not lined up |
| --- | --- |
| Text: "Future of home design", then "Life-size floor plans" with an arrow. Shot: people walking over a floor plan projected at full size. Said: "These are life-size floor plans. You can walk through your home before you build it." | Text: "Future of storytelling". Shot: rooms appearing out of nowhere. Said: "It's called a generative world model. It turns a single image into a photorealistic cinematic world." Abstract, full of jargon, nothing on screen that shows it. |

When the shot can't show the claim, change the claim ("These rooms are the future of
commercials", with footage of a shoot in them) or drop the idea.

## Text hook

Often the strongest layer: eyes go to text and pick out key words faster than they read an
image.

**What it says.** The subject's name, plus one of:

- the pain it solves ("hook mistakes"),
- the outcome the viewer wants ("unskippable hooks"),
- a change in the field ("Claude just changed social media").

**How it is written:**

- Three to five words, large and bold.
- Specific numbers: "22 days", not "a few weeks".
- Strong short phrases over flat ones: "heat-seeking missiles", not "good for attention".
- Two lines at most. Break where the phrase reads naturally: "ChatGPT just pioneered / the
  future of work", never "ChatGPT just / pioneered the future of work". Three lines only when
  one line is much bigger than the others.
- No punctuation, except quotation marks and parentheses.
- No joining words: and, or, that, but, therefore. It is not a sentence.
- When the name of the thing could be misread, lead with what it means: "Future of home
  design" before "Life-size floor plans".

**Where and how long:**

- About a third down from the top, where the eyes land first. Never over the eyes or mouth.
- On screen for about three seconds: long enough to read, look at the picture, and read
  again.
- Plain system font, apart from the captions, or a designed one. Look at the top creators in
  the niche to choose a look, then make it the user's own.

## Spoken hook

The formula is in [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md). Two more things to work on:

- **Templates.** The grammar of hooks that work, with the topic taken out. See
  [formats.md](formats.md).
- **Power words.** Short phrases that grab attention and carry the outcome or the pain:
  "social media machine", "scientifically impossible to skip". When one shows up in several
  of the user's best hooks, it works for their audience. Remix it ("psychologically impossible
  to ignore", "sales machine") and combine it with a template.

## Visual hook

Five kinds:

1. Graphics or text over the shot.
2. A switch: cut from the speaker to something else.
3. The speaker moves: into or out of frame, closer, an arm, a turn.
4. An effect or a transition on the footage.
5. A striking shot chosen on purpose.

Enough motion to catch the eye, not so much that the viewer can't follow. The easy 80/20:
move in the frame, move the shot, or move text over the shot. Small moves; tricks look
amateur when they are forced.

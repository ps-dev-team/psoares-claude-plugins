# The lock-in zone

The one to four sentences right after the hook, the next five to ten seconds. The hook makes
the viewer curious; the lock-in zone makes them stay. Some videos go straight from the hook to
the body, but most hold better with one of the methods below.

Two things happen in it every time:

1. **The hook is confirmed.** The video is about what the hook promised. No bait and switch.
2. **The viewer decides whether to trust you.** A strong claim is not enough; they check
   whether you will deliver. Proof matters most. Care in the production, and a speaker who
   feels like a fit for this viewer, help too.

## Six methods

### 1. Tease the proof

A bold claim in the hook, concrete proof in the next line.

> You can now have Claude optimise the calls to action in your videos.
> I ran this once and my leads per video went up four times.

Use it only with real proof: a number, a result, a client. Prefer it over the others when
there is proof.

### 2. Tease the transformation

The change the viewer wants, when it can't be measured.

> Here's how to write a hook so good it's basically unskippable.
> It's called the desire hook, and it turns viewers into buyers.

Use it when there is no hard proof but the outcome the viewer wants is clear.

### 3. Rapid contrast

State what everyone believes, then the alternative.

> The reason your videos don't hold attention is the story arc.
> In school we all learned this one: conflict, rising action, climax, resolution.
> But online, most people scroll away before the rising action.
> So use this instead: the modern arc.

Use it when the hook's topic is a little flat on its own.

### 4. Name the method

Give the method a name the viewer doesn't know yet, and explain it later.

> This is called the dopamine addiction loop.
> It's only four steps.

Use it when there is a method or framework to name. It stacks well with the others.

### 5. Answer the objection

Say the viewer's main "this isn't for me" before they think it.

> Everybody loves talking-head videos because they look easy to make.
> But the irony is the best ones all follow the same formula.

> Here are four videos that will make your content better.
> And the catch: none of them are about social media.

Use it when the claim is bold and there is an obvious objection. With no objection, use
another method.

### 6. Double down on the claim

Say the claim again, in other words.

> What you're seeing isn't Formula 1, because those cars have no driver.
> It's an autonomous racing league, where the cars drive themselves.

Use it when the claim is new or easy to miss, or when one word carries the whole video and has
to land twice.

## Long-form

In a long video, the lock-in zone is the rest of the intro: confirm the title and the
thumbnail, give the map of the video, and a short "who I am" with real proof. Same two jobs,
more room.

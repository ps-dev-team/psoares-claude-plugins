# Frameworks

Two ways to look at the same thing. The **loop** is what to write. The **ladder** is how a
viewer moves through a video, and helps when reviewing why people drop off.

## The loop

### Stakes

Turns the viewer's guessing on. Without stakes, there is nothing to predict and nothing holds
them.

- **A character.** A real person in a real situation: the speaker, a client, "a creator I
  spoke to last week". It does not need to be fictional.
- **Something at risk.** What failure looks like: "I was about to lose my biggest client", "the
  whole launch was on the line".
- **Urgency.** A clock. "Within a week" lands harder than "eventually". "The call was in two
  hours" lands harder than "later that day".

Check: before the story starts, could the viewer say who this is about, what is at risk, and
why it matters now? If not, add it. Skipping the stakes is the most common mistake.

### Big question

The question the viewer chews on through the story. Load it as early as possible: the earlier
it lands, the longer it holds.

- Give enough specific detail to guess from, not so much that it overwhelms.
- Ask it outright, or imply it with details so the viewer asks it themselves.
- It has to matter to this viewer. A huge question on a topic they don't care about loses them
  at once.
- Two ways to sharpen it: contrast something known with something unknown, or open with a
  premise they have never heard.

Before any important reveal: stop, back up, and give the context the viewer needs to make a
guess first. The reveal needs a guess to land against.

| Flat | With a question |
| --- | --- |
| I had a call with the client and it went well. | Thirty minutes into the call, she told me something that changed my plan for the next six months. |
| Something crazy happened. | We were about to cancel the product, until five thousand pastel cups sold out in five days. |

### Head fake

Break the guess the viewer just made.

1. Work out what a normal viewer expects from what has been said so far.
2. Reveal something that contradicts it and that they accept as true straight away.

A good head fake is surprising in the moment and obvious looking back, because the clues were
there. A magician shows the empty hat before the rabbit. A cheap twist comes from nowhere, does
not connect to the setup, and leaves the viewer confused instead of hooked.

The bigger the gap between the guess and the reveal, the stronger it lands, as long as it still
makes sense.

### Re-hook

Close one question and open the next in the same breath. The places where one part ends and the
next begins are where attention drops: put a new question, a complication or a consequence
right there.

| Dead air | Re-hook |
| --- | --- |
| She doubled her budget, and that was a great call. Anyway, next… | And that's when she told me she was doubling her budget, which would have been great if I hadn't signed with her competitor the night before. |

Joins that close and open at once:

- "which would have been great, except…"
- "but here's the problem with that…"
- "and that's when I realised…"
- "which is exactly why…"

In teaching content, the re-hook is often a new problem: solve the first one, then "but even
with this fixed, it won't work unless you also…". After three or four of these, the viewer
feels several problems were solved, and they stayed for all of them.

## The ladder

Six stages a viewer climbs. A review can walk a script through the first four to find where
people leave.

| Stage | What the viewer goes through | What the content needs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Stimulation | The first one or two seconds: colour, motion, contrast stop the scroll before anything is understood | A first frame that looks unlike the rest of the feed, in the creator's own look |
| 2. Captivation | A question pops into their head | A question that is both intriguing and relevant to this viewer |
| 3. Anticipation | They start guessing the answer; attention peaks just before it | Clear facts that move them closer, then a head fake or a new question |
| 4. Validation | The question closes | A payoff they did not expect: the resolution, or the actual tip |
| 5. Affection | They start to like the creator | A person to like: warmth, a smile, and above all real help with their problem |
| 6. Revelation | They learn the creator is a steady source of value | Useful, non-obvious content, again and again |

Stages 1 to 4 belong to one video. Stages 5 and 6 build across many, and are easier for teaching
content than for entertainment. They are advice for the channel, not rules for one script.

If a hook stops the scroll but people still leave, the question is the problem: it was not
interesting enough, or not relevant to them.

## The modern arc

The school arc (scene, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution) builds too slowly
for online content: most viewers leave before the rising action gets interesting.

The arc that holds attention:

1. Open at about 70 out of 100: the intro already carries tension.
2. First conflict or contrast early, up to about 90.
3. Release, down to about 30.
4. Build to the next peak, a conflict or a head fake, at about 75.
5. Release again, and repeat every two to five minutes in long-form.

The biggest peak is either the first, with the others stepping down, or the last, with every
peak leading up to it.

## The hook order

| Old order | Modern order |
| --- | --- |
| When and where: "Last month I was at a conference in Miami." | What and why: "The future of entertainment is going to look very different." |
| Who and what: "I saw the CEO of Disney speak." | Who and how: "The CEO of Disney is worried about it." |
| How and why: "He's worried about regulation." | When and where: "He said so at a conference in Miami." |

What and why let the viewer decide the story is for them. Who and how make it credible. When
and where are only colour: lead with the cake, not the icing. Assume every viewer has a timer
of a few seconds before they decide.

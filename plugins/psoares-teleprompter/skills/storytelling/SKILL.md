# Storytelling

A story holds attention while the viewer has a question they want answered. This skill builds
the structure that keeps one open: what the story is about, why it matters now, what the viewer
expects, and how each answer opens the next question.

It does the structure. The words on each line come from the skills that own them:

- **`video-script`** for scripts recorded one sentence per line.
- **`human-prose`** (from `s2-writing` or `psoares-writing`) for the voice.
- **`teleprompter-script`** when the script is read from the Markdown Teleprompter app.

## Modes

- **Write.** From a topic or notes to a story. Follow "Building a story" below.
- **Rewrite.** A flat story into a looped one. Keep every fact, change the order and the
  joins. [references/examples.md](references/examples.md) has a full flat and looped pair.
- **Review.** Go through a script with [references/review.md](references/review.md) and report
  what breaks, line by line. Ask before changing a script the user wrote: their voice wins.

## Before writing, ask

Ask only what the notes do not already answer.

1. Who is the viewer, and what do they care about?
2. What is the format: a short loop, long-form, an email, a sales call?
3. What is the character, the risk, and the deadline? It can be the speaker or a client.
4. What visuals are there? A screen, b-roll, the speaker only?

## Building a story

1. **Pick the lens.** List the angles most people would take on the topic, then push to the
   least common one that still fits the viewer. A good topic is not enough when many people
   cover it.
2. **Write the last line first.** It should be worth repeating to a friend on its own. In a
   short loop, it also leads back into the first line when the video replays.
3. **Write the first line.** It says what the story is about, plainly. No opaque teaser.
4. **Set the stakes** before the story starts: who it happens to, what is at risk, why now.
5. **Load the big question** early, then run the loop through the middle. See below.
6. **Close the main question** with a payoff the viewer did not see coming.
7. **Check it** against [references/review.md](references/review.md).

## The loop

Every story that holds attention runs this loop, over and over. Detail and examples for each
step are in [references/frameworks.md](references/frameworks.md).

1. **Stakes.** A character the viewer can root for, something at risk, and urgency. Stakes do
   not need to be life or death, only relevant to this viewer: "I had 48 hours to find out
   why our ads weren't working, or the whole budget was gone."
2. **Big question.** Give just enough specific detail for the viewer to guess the answer.
   "Something crazy happened" gives them nothing to guess. "Within 30 minutes she told me
   something that changed my plan for the next six months" does.
3. **Head fake.** Work out what the viewer now expects, then reveal something else that is
   clearly true once said. The clues were there all along. A twist that comes from nowhere
   only confuses.
4. **Re-hook.** Open the next question in the same sentence that closes the last one, so
   there is never a moment where the viewer thinks "OK, done".

## Rules for every story

- **Join beats with "but" or "therefore", never "and then".** "And then" piles up detail.
  "But" adds conflict and "therefore" adds consequence. (The rule is Trey Parker and Matt
  Stone's.)
- **Lead with what and why.** The intro runs what is happening and why it matters, then who
  says so and how they know, then when and where, which is only colour.
- **Open high.** The first lines carry tension already. The first conflict comes early, not
  after a slow build.
- **Give the viewer a villain.** It can be a thing, a group, a habit, or a rival way of doing
  it: "They do this, but we do that."
- **Give them someone to root for.** Say why the story matters to this viewer, so they see
  themselves in the hero.
- **Make it retellable.** The core idea fits in one sentence a viewer could repeat after one
  watch. For a complex point, say it twice: once plainly, once with a metaphor a child would
  get.
- **Show while you tell.** Plan a visual for the hook and for each main point.
- **Vary sentence length.** Short, medium and long, so it sounds like speech and not a list.
  With one sentence per line, the right edge of the script should look ragged.
- **Talk to one person.** Write it like a voice note to a close friend.

## Length and format

- **Short-form (under about 90 seconds).** One stakes setup, one big question, one or two head
  fakes, and a last line that loops into the first. Several "but" turns in the first 30
  seconds.
- **Long-form.** Peaks and releases: open around high intensity, first conflict within the
  first minute or two, release, build to the next peak. A new peak every two to five minutes.
  After each answer, set up a new problem that the viewer has to stay for.
- **Emails, posts and sales calls.** The same loop, with fewer turns. Stakes and the big
  question in the first lines, one head fake, and a closing line that asks for the next step.

## What not to do

- Do not invent facts, numbers or events to make a head fake work. If the material has no
  surprise, find a better lens or a sharper question instead.
- Do not leave the main question open at the end. Smaller loops can carry into the next video;
  the main one closes.
- Do not cite the frameworks' brain science as fact in the content itself. The mechanics here
  are craft heuristics.
